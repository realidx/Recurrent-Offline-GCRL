\begin{abstract}
Offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL) enables goal-reaching from static datasets, but the learned value, critic, or compatibility score must support both long-horizon reachability inference and offline policy extraction.
We study \emph{iterative latent refinement} as an architecture-level replacement for the feedforward value or critic backbone used by existing offline GCRL algorithms.
Instead of emitting a score after a single feedforward computation, the network repeatedly updates a latent representation using shared recurrent update weights and lightweight step-specific parameters before exposing the final actor-facing signal.
On the recent benchmark datasets reported in this study, recurrent variants improve performance across several algorithms without changing their losses, data, actors, or training protocol; across the 21 reported algorithm-dataset rows, the mean improvement is $10.7$ percentage points, with the largest gains on stitching and bottleneck maze tasks.
CRL diagnostics are consistent with an improved policy-extraction interface: recurrent critics increase normalized separation between matched and mismatched goals and produce actors that remain closer to dataset actions under several behavior-support proxies.
Depth and update-capacity studies further suggest that the effect depends on how computation is allocated between recurrent depth and per-step expressivity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning aims to learn policies that can solve many goals from shared experience \citep{uvfa, her, gcsl}. In the offline setting, the learner must infer goal-reaching behavior from a fixed dataset, without online interaction to correct value errors, collect missing transitions, or recover from out-of-distribution policy updates \citep{cql, iql, td3bc}. OGBench highlights these difficulties through tasks that probe trajectory stitching, long-horizon reasoning, state-goal generalization, high-dimensional inputs, stochasticity, and policy extraction from offline data \citep{ogbench}.

Prior offline GCRL methods address different parts of this problem. Contrastive and quasimetric methods learn reachability or compatibility signals \citep{crl, qrl, cgcivl}; supervised, advantage-weighted, and bootstrapped methods emphasize policy extraction from fixed data \citep{wgcsL, gofar, dawog, saw}; and hierarchical or horizon-reduction methods use subgoals, temporal abstraction, or multi-step targets to reduce long-horizon difficulty \citep{hiql, ota, horizon_reduction}. These directions also point to different bottlenecks: faraway-goal values can be hard to estimate \citep{hiql}, high-level advantage estimates can have incorrect signs in long-horizon regimes \citep{ota}, cross-trajectory stitching can overestimate unconnected state-goal pairs \citep{cgcivl}, and offline RL performance can be limited by policy extraction or generalization rather than value learning alone \citep{value_bottleneck}. Thus, we do not treat ``value learning bottleneck'' as a single failure mode.

We instead focus on the learned signal that connects static trajectory evidence to policy improvement. Across offline GCRL algorithms, a value, critic, or compatibility network must encode long-horizon reachability, avoid unsupported optimism, and expose a signal usable by the chosen actor-extraction procedure \citep{ogbench, value_bottleneck}. In CRL this interface is a contrastive score consumed by a DDPG+BC-style actor \citep{crl, td3bc}; in HIQL it is a scalar value used for hierarchical policy learning \citep{hiql}; and in SAW it enters a subgoal-weighted actor update \citep{saw}. We ask whether changing the architecture of this learned signal, while leaving the surrounding algorithm fixed, can make the policy-extraction interface more useful.

Standard implementations of these methods commonly use feedforward MLP backbones for the value, critic, or compatibility network \citep{crl, hiql, ogbench, saw}. At the same time, architecture and compute allocation have become important levers in RL and representation learning, with depth, residual structure, regularization, and explicit compute scaling improving several settings \citep{resnet, d2rl, dr3, bro, simba, thousand_layers}. We therefore study recurrent latent refinement: instead of producing a score in one feedforward pass, the network repeatedly revises a latent representation before exposing the final actor-facing signal. This follows models that allocate computation through repeated latent updates or implicit depth \citep{universal_transformer, act, deq, recurrent_relational_networks, energy_diffusion, hrm, trm, urm}. Section~\ref{sec:architecture} gives the detailed design, including SwiGLU updates, step embeddings, LayerNorm, and LayerScale \citep{shazeer_glu, universal_transformer, layer_norm, layerscale}. We use this module as an architecture-level replacement for the MLP backbone in CRL, HIQL, and SAW, keeping losses, data, actors, and optimization unchanged. Because CRL exposes its compatibility score directly to the actor \citep{crl}, we use it as the diagnostic case for measuring score separation and behavior-support proxies.


Our contributions are threefold.
\textbf{First}, we introduce recurrent latent refinement as a value-network architecture for offline GCRL and instantiate it in different algorithms without changing their objectives or actors.
\textbf{Second}, we report empirical gains on a subset of OGBench locomotion and manipulation tasks, with large improvements on stitching and bottleneck mazes.
\textbf{Third}, we provide CRL diagnostics, depth studies, and ablations suggesting that the gains are tied to the actor-facing score and to the allocation of computation between iterative depth and per-step expressivity.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning.}
Goal-conditioned RL learns policies and value functions that generalize across desired outcomes, building on goal-indexed value approximation, temporal abstraction, hindsight relabeling, and supervised goal reaching \citep{uvfa, options, feudal_rl, her, gcsl}. Later methods learn goal-conditioned distances, future-state classifiers, latent goal spaces, replay-buffer plans, or contrastive scores for long-horizon and visual control \citep{tdm, rig, sorb, skewfit, c_learning, crl}. These works motivate goal-conditioned values and future-state scores as control signals, but most assume online or self-supervised data collection rather than a fixed dataset.

\paragraph{Offline goal-conditioned RL algorithms.}
Offline RL must learn from fixed data while avoiding value errors on out-of-distribution actions, motivating conservative or behavior-regularized methods such as CQL, IQL, TD3+BC, and SAC+BC \citep{cql, iql, td3, td3bc, sac}. Offline GCRL adds state-goal generalization and trajectory stitching, so prior work has attacked different failure modes. Some methods improve policy extraction from fixed data through supervised or advantage-weighted learning, including the OGBench GCBC/GCIVL/GCIQL baselines, WGCSL, GoFAR, DAWOG, and SAW \citep{ogbench, wgcsL, gofar, dawog, saw}. Others focus on the learned reachability signal itself: CRL uses contrastive compatibility learning, while QRL and CGCIVL impose quasimetric or conservative value structure to reduce errors on poorly connected state-goal pairs \citep{crl, qrl, cgcivl}. A third line reduces the effective horizon through temporal abstraction, subgoals, or multi-step value learning, from classical options to HIQL, OTA, and SHARSA \citep{options, hiql, ota, horizon_reduction}. OGBench evaluates these issues through stitching, long-horizon, stochastic, visual, and policy-extraction tasks \citep{ogbench}, while recent analysis shows that value learning, policy extraction, and generalization can each be bottlenecks \citep{value_bottleneck}. We keep losses, data, and actors fixed, and instead study the computation inside the critic.

\paragraph{Architecture and iterative computation.}
Although deep RL often uses shallow MLPs, architecture and regularization matter: dense or residual designs, explicit TD regularizers, plasticity methods, and dormant-neuron recycling all target representation quality or network utilization \citep{resnet, d2rl, dr3, implicit_underparam, primacy_bias, dormant_neurons}. Larger regularized actor-critic backbones and very deep self-supervised RL networks further show that capacity can change control performance \citep{bro, simba, thousand_layers}. A natural next step is to ask not only how much computation a critic has, but how that computation is organized. Repeated latent updates appear in Neural Turing machines, Neural GPUs, adaptive computation, Universal Transformers, and equilibrium models \citep{ntm, neural_gpu, act, universal_transformer, deq}; related recurrent, Hopfield, deep-thinking, energy-diffusion, and compact reasoning models show how iterative correction can support structured prediction and reasoning \citep{recurrent_relational_networks, modern_hopfield, deep_thinking, energy_diffusion, hrm, trm, urm}. We use normalization, gating, and residual-scale control common in deep repeated computation \citep{layer_norm, shazeer_glu, layerscale}, adapting this pattern to offline GCRL critics whose signals must support both reachability inference and behavior-constrained policy extraction.




\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
\paragraph{Offline goal-conditioned RL}
We consider an offline goal-conditioned Markov decision process with state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition kernel $P$, and goal space $\mathcal{G}$. The learner is given a fixed dataset of trajectories $\mathcal{D}=\{\tau^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}$, with $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,\ldots,s_T)$, and receives no environment interaction during training. Given an initial state and goal $g$, the objective is to learn a goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a\mid s,g)$ that maximizes the discounted goal-reaching return
\(
J(\pi)=
\mathbb{E}_{g \sim p(g),\, \tau \sim p^{\pi}(\tau \mid g)}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_g(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})
\right],
\)
where $r_g$ is the sparse goal-reaching reward or its environment-specific analogue. During training, goals are sampled from the dataset, with future states providing positive reachability supervision and other dataset states providing contrastive comparisons.

\paragraph{Contrastive RL}
Contrastive RL (CRL) learns a goal-conditioned compatibility score and then extracts a policy from that score \citep{crl}. We write the critic as $f_{\phi,\psi}(s,a,g)$ and the actor as $\pi_\theta(a\mid s,g)$. CRL can be written as a binary-NCE objective that contrasts a future goal $g$ against a random goal $g^-$ \citep{ma_collins_binary_nce, crl}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{CRL}}(f)
=
\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim p_{\mathcal{D}},\,g\sim p_{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathrm{future}}(\cdot\mid s,a),\,g^-\sim p_{\mathcal{D}}}
\left[
\log\sigma(f(s,a,g))+\log(1-\sigma(f(s,a,g^-)))
\right].
\end{equation}
In our experiments, the score is parameterized as a scaled inner product
\(f_{\phi,\psi}(s,a,g)=\phi(s,a)^\top\psi(g)/\sqrt{d}\).
For policy extraction, continuous-action CRL uses the DDPG+BC objective from OGBench, replacing $Q$ with the learned CRL score:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{actor}}(\pi_\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim p_{\mathcal{D}},\,g\sim p_{\mathcal{D}}(\cdot\mid s)}
\left[
f_{\phi,\psi}(s,\pi_\theta^\mu(s,g),g)
+ \alpha \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s,g)
\right],
\end{equation}
where $\pi_\theta^\mu(s,g)$ is the mean action of the policy \citep{td3bc, ogbench}. Thus policy extraction depends directly on the learned score while remaining regularized toward behavior support. This direct score-to-actor interface is why CRL is our main diagnostic setting for studying how refinement changes value learning. We use the same CRL setup as OGBench to keep the comparison straightforward.

\paragraph{Iterative refinement}
Given an input $x$, let $\rho$ and $R$ denote the input and output encoder that maps $x$ to an initial latent state and maps final latent state to the prediction. A recurrent-depth model initializes $h^{(0)}=\rho(x)$ and applies
\begin{equation}
h^{(k+1)}
=
h^{(k)} + F_{\theta}(h^{(k)}, x, e_k),
\qquad
k=0,\ldots,K-1,
\end{equation}
before predicting $\hat y = R(h^{(K)})$. The channel update parameters $\theta$ are shared across refinement steps, so depth is obtained by recurrently applying the same learned transformation rather than by stacking independent layers. This form of computation appears in Universal Transformers, adaptive computation methods, deep equilibrium models, and recurrent reasoning systems \citep{universal_transformer, act, deq, recurrent_relational_networks, energy_diffusion, hrm, trm, urm}. In practice, repeated updates are sensitive to optimization and scale. Step embeddings $e_k$ let a shared recurrent block distinguish refinement stages \citep{universal_transformer}, while normalization and residual scaling, such as LayerNorm and LayerScale, help stabilize deep or repeated computation \citep{layer_norm, layerscale}.

Figure~\ref{fig:refinement-detailed} illustrates the recurrent refinement block used as our backbone. The module replaces a feedforward value or critic block while keeping the surrounding algorithm, losses, replay data, and optimization procedure unchanged. Given an input representation $x$, it initializes a latent state $h^{(0)}$ and refines it through $K$ outer iterations. Each outer iteration performs $m$ inner update blocks before exposing the next latent state, so $K$ controls how many times the representation is revised, while $m$ controls how expressive each revision is. Each inner block uses pre-layer normalization, a learned outer-step embedding, a SwiGLU channel update, and residual computation \citep{layer_norm, universal_transformer, shazeer_glu}. Let $h^{(k,0)}=h^{(k)}$ denote the start of outer refinement step $k$. For inner block $j=1,\ldots,m$, we compute
\begin{equation}
h^{(k,j)}
=
h^{(k,j-1)}
+
W_{o,j}\,\mathrm{SwiGLU}_{j}
\!\left(\mathrm{LN}_{j}(h^{(k,j-1)}) + E_k\right),
\end{equation}
where $E_k$ is a learned embedding for the outer refinement step. The inner block weights are reused across outer iterations. Thus increasing $m$ increases the capacity of a single refinement update without changing the number of outer revisions. After the $m$ inner updates, a learned per-step LayerScale coefficient controls how much of the proposed update is accepted:
\begin{equation}
h^{(k+1)}
=
h^{(k)}
+\alpha_k\left(h^{(k,m)}-h^{(k)}\right),
\qquad k=0,\ldots,K-1.
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha_k$ is initialized small and learned separately for each outer step, stabilizing repeated residual computation \citep{layerscale}. The case $m=1$ recovers a single-update refinement block, while $m>1$ gives a more expressive stacked-SwiGLU update within each refinement step. This design lets us vary repeated revision depth and per-step update capacity separately before the algorithm consumes the resulting value, score, or policy signal.


\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Experiment Setup}\label{section:exp-setup}
\paragraph{Environments}
We evaluate on OGBench \citep{ogbench}, a benchmark designed specifically for offline goal-conditioned RL. In this setting the agent receives a fixed dataset of trajectories and must learn to reach new evaluation goals without any additional environment interaction. OGBench provides standardized datasets and multiple held-out state-goal evaluation pairs for each task, making it useful for testing long-horizon reachability, state-goal stitching, and policy extraction from offline data. The benchmark spans locomotion and manipulation domains: locomotion tasks require simulated agents to navigate mazes of different sizes and dataset types, while manipulation tasks require reaching object configurations or compositional scene goals. Following the benchmark protocol, we report average success over the five OGBench evaluation tasks for each dataset, with 50 episodes per task.


\paragraph{Algorithms and architecture}
We study recurrent latent refinement as an architecture-level replacement inside existing offline GCRL algorithms, rather than as a new loss or data-generation procedure. Our experiments include CRL, HIQL, and SAW as the main algorithmic settings. These methods expose different value-to-policy interfaces: CRL learns a contrastive state-action-goal score and extracts the actor with a DDPG+BC-style objective \citep{crl, td3bc}; HIQL uses hierarchical value learning with AWR-style actor extraction \citep{hiql}; and SAW uses a different policy-extraction procedure based on its own subgoal-weighted training objective \citep{saw}. In each comparison, the recurrent variant replaces only the value or critic backbone that produces the learned signal consumed by the actor; the actor networks themselves remain feedforward. The surrounding algorithm is otherwise held fixed, including replay data, goal sampling, losses, batch size, optimizer, and evaluation protocol. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation-details} specifies the replacement location for each algorithm and the recurrent hyperparameters.

\subsection{Effect of Refinement Depth}
\label{sec:depth}
The main results use a fixed refinement depth, but the benefit of recurrence should depend on whether repeated updates provide useful additional computation. We therefore sweep the number of outer refinement steps $K$ in CRL on AMS and AGN, comparing the MLP baseline with recurrent critics using $K\in\{1,2,4,6,8\}$. Figure~\ref{fig:iter_ams_agn} shows a clear depth-dependent pattern. A single recurrent update is insufficient, increasing the number of refinement steps produces a sharp improvement, but the gains then saturate rather than increasing monotonically, suggesting that recurrent refinement is useful up to the point where the latent representation has resolved the relevant reachability structure, after which additional updates provide limited benefit and can introduce optimization noise. We use $K=4$ in the main comparisons because it lies in this high-performing regime while keeping training and evaluation cost moderate. Detailed numeric results for the AMS depth sweep are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:iter}.

\subsection{What Does the Recurrent Critic Change?}\label{section:why better}
End-task success alone does not explain what recurrent refinement changes. We therefore use CRL as the main diagnostic setting because its critic signal is exposed directly: CRL learns a compatibility score $f(s,a,g)$ and the DDPG+BC actor is trained to choose actions with high score under this critic. This lets us evaluate the learned signal at the policy-extraction interface, rather than only through final rollout success. We focus on three state-based AntMaze tasks: AMS, ALS, and AGN. Unless stated otherwise, the comparison is between the default MLP CRL critic and the four-step recurrent CRL critic. The diagnostic tables report mean $\pm$ std over four seeds. We ask three diagnostic questions. First, how does recurrence change the critic's discrimination between matched future goals and sampled negative goals? Second, do actor-interface proxies suggest a less extrapolative policy extraction step? Third, does maze-distance alignment improve uniformly enough to explain the control gains by itself?

For critic discrimination, we use the contrastive structure of CRL. A dataset transition paired with a future goal from the same trajectory is treated as a positive pair, while pairing the same transition with a sampled goal gives a negative pair. Let $q^+=f(s,a,g)$ and $q^-=f(s,a,g^-)$ denote these matched and mismatched scores. We report the raw margin $\mathbb{E}[q^+-q^-]$, ranking accuracy $\Pr[q^+>q^-]$, and normalized separation \(\mathrm{Sep}= \frac{\mathbb{E}[q^+-q^-]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(q^+) + \mathrm{Var}(q^-) + \epsilon}}\)
This statistic is an effect-size-style normalization of score separation; it is used as a diagnostic summary rather than a calibrated estimate of reachability.
For actor-interface diagnostics, we compare the critic scores of the extracted actor action and the dataset action on the same state-goal pairs:
\(
q_\Delta
=
\mathbb{E}_{(s,a_{\mathcal D},g)}
\left[
f(s,\pi^\mu(s,g),g)-f(s,a_{\mathcal D},g)
\right]
\)
We also report the fraction of pairs where the critic prefers the actor action, policy--behavior MSE/L2 distance, and the actor log probability of the dataset action, $\mathbb{E}[\log \pi(a_{\mathcal D}\mid s,g)]$. Lower $q_\Delta$ and smaller policy--behavior distance are proxies for less extrapolative actor extraction, but they are not a causal test of behavior support; in particular, $q_\Delta$ is used as a within-critic action-preference diagnostic rather than a calibrated quantity across critics. Finally, graph-distance correlations test whether improved alignment with a shortest-path surrogate is a plausible sole explanation.

\paragraph{Critic discrimination changes in normalized form.}
Across AMS, ALS, and AGN, recurrent refinement improves normalized separation between matched and mismatched goals (Figure~\ref{fig:success_sep_graphcorr}(a,b); full statistics in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic}). Because rank accuracy is already high on several tasks, we treat the separation metric as the more informative critic-side diagnostic. The result suggests that recurrence changes the score distribution beyond a simple increase in raw score scale.

\subsection{Matched Parameter and Computation Comparison}\label{matched}
A natural alternative explanation is that the recurrent critic improves simply because it has more trainable parameters than the default CRL critic. We test this explanation with feedforward controls that preserve CRL's dense critic recipe, including GELU activations and layer normalization, while matching the recurrent critic's parameter count by either increasing width or increasing depth.
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.48\textwidth}
    \vspace{-0.9em}

The two controls are a wider three hidden layer critic with hidden size \texttt{688} and a deeper five hidden layer critic with hidden size \texttt{512}; their parameter counts are within 0.37\% and 0.19\% of the recurrent critic, respectively. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:parameter_match}, neither feedforward alternative closes the gap to the recurrent critic. The gain is therefore not explained by parameter count alone. Instead, the result supports a compute-allocation view: recurrent refinement reuses a shared update over multiple latent steps, whereas the dense controls spend comparable capacity in a single feedforward computation. Appendix~\ref{app:compute-comparison} reports the corresponding wall-clock overhead for the recurrent critic.


\subsection{Ablation on Model Components}
We isolate the components of the recurrent update while keeping the CRL objective and refinement depth fixed. Each variant preserves the recurrent critic but removes one architectural choice: the refinement-step embedding, pre-normalization in the update block, LayerScale on the residual update, or the SwiGLU update MLP.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/ablation2.pdf}
    \caption{Component ablations for the recurrent CRL critic on AMS and ALS.}
    \label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} shows that the complete recurrent update is the most reliable configuration across both tasks. Removing step conditioning weakens the model's ability to distinguish early and late refinement passes, while removing normalization or residual scaling makes the repeated update less stable. Replacing SwiGLU with a dense update also reduces performance, suggesting that the gain is not due to recurrence alone but to a controlled update block that can refine the critic representation over multiple steps. 

\section{Conclusion}
Reinforcement learning has often relied on comparatively shallow value and policy networks, and recent work has begun to challenge this convention by scaling depth, width, and regularization in RL architectures \citep{thousand_layers, bro, simba}. Our results suggest a complementary way to allocate computation: rather than only making the feedforward critic larger, offline GCRL can benefit from recurrently refining the value representation before policy extraction, reminiscent of recurrence in the brain~\citep{kietzmann2019cortical}. This connects value learning to recurrent-depth and iterative reasoning models, where repeated latent updates support structured inference \citep{universal_transformer, deq, recurrent_relational_networks, energy_diffusion, hrm, trm, urm}. In offline GCRL, the critic must infer long-horizon reachability from fixed data and provide an actor-usable signal \citep{ogbench, value_bottleneck}; recurrent refinement improves this interface by producing a more discriminative and actor-friendly signal, especially on stitching and bottleneck tasks. Because the same architectural principle improves the reported algorithms with different training regimes without changing their objectives or data, we view iterative refinement as a practical design axis for offline RL that complements losses, datasets, and policy-extraction methods.


\paragraph{Limitations.}\label{limitation}
Our study is empirical and focused on offline GCRL, primarily on OGBench. Because recurrent variants are more expensive to train than single-pass critics, and due to time and compute constraints, we do not exhaustively cover the full OGBench suite, all algorithms, or all hyperparameter regimes. The best recurrent configuration is also not universal: refinement depth $K$ and inner update capacity $m$ interact with the algorithm and environment, so recurrent computation must be allocated deliberately rather than scaled blindly. A useful next step is to develop selection rules that predict a good balance between outer refinement depth and per-step update capacity before training. Adaptive computation is another natural direction, but applying mechanisms such as ACT \citep{act} to GCRL is nontrivial because there is no direct per-example halting signal during value learning. Finally, our mechanism analysis is diagnostic rather than causal, and the present experiments leave open the boundary of where iterative refinement helps. Future work should test broader offline and online RL settings, combine recurrent refinement with complementary algorithmic improvements, and develop a more principled account of when allocating computation through iterative value refinement is most beneficial.

\section{Algorithm Background: HIQL and SAW}
\label{app:algorithm-background}

\paragraph{HIQL.}
HIQL decomposes long-horizon goal reaching into high-level subgoal prediction and low-level action generation \citep{hiql}.
Let $V_\psi(s,g)$ denote the scalar goal-conditioned value, $\pi_{\ell}(a\mid s,z)$ the low-level actor conditioned on a subgoal representation $z$, and $\pi_h(z\mid s,g)$ the high-level actor that predicts such a representation for the final goal $g$.
Suppressing ensemble details, the value module is trained with an expectile implicit-value loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{V}^{\mathrm{HIQL}}
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\ell_\tau\!\left(
r_g(s,a,s')+\gamma M V_{\bar\psi}(s',g)-V_\psi(s,g)
\right)
\right],
\qquad
\ell_\tau(u)=|\tau-\mathbf{1}\{u<0\}|u^2,
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the dataset mask and $V_{\bar\psi}$ is a target value network.
The actors are trained with advantage-weighted behavioral cloning. For a low-level goal $g_\ell$ and a high-level target state $w$, the advantages are
\begin{equation}
A_\ell=V_\psi(s',g_\ell)-V_\psi(s,g_\ell),
\qquad
A_h=V_\psi(w,g)-V_\psi(s,g).
\end{equation}
The corresponding actor losses can be written as
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{HIQL}}
&=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\alpha_\ell A_\ell)\log \pi_\ell(a\mid s,\phi(s,g_\ell))\right],
\\
\mathcal{L}_{h}^{\mathrm{HIQL}}
&=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\alpha_h A_h)\log \pi_h(\phi(s,w)\mid s,g)\right],
\end{align}
with the implementation clipping the exponential weights for stability.
In our experiments, recurrent refinement replaces only $V_\psi$ and its target backbone; the goal representation $\phi$, high-level actor, low-level actor, losses, and sampling scheme remain unchanged.

\paragraph{SAW.}
SAW also trains a scalar goal-conditioned value with an expectile loss, but uses it to bootstrap a flat policy through subgoal-weighted objectives \citep{saw}.
Let $\pi_\ell(a\mid s,w)$ be the low-level actor for an intermediate target $w$, and let $\pi(a\mid s,g)$ be the flat actor for the final goal.
Using the same value target form as above, SAW trains the low-level and flat actors with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SAW}}
=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\alpha_\ell A_\ell)\log \pi_\ell(a\mid s,w)\right],
\qquad
A_\ell=V_\psi(s',w)-V_\psi(s,w),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\pi}^{\mathrm{SAW}}
=
-\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\alpha_{\mathrm{awr}} A_\pi)\log \pi(a\mid s,g)\right],
\qquad
A_\pi=V_\psi(s',g)-V_\psi(s,g).
\end{equation}
SAW further adds a waypoint-consistency term that encourages the flat actor to match the low-level actor at value-improving intermediate targets:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wp}}^{\mathrm{SAW}}
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\exp(\beta A_w)\,
D\!\left(\pi(\cdot\mid s,g),\,\pi_\ell(\cdot\mid s,w)\right)
\right],
\qquad
A_w=V_\psi(w,g)-V_\psi(s,g),
\end{equation}
where $D$ is implemented as a KL-style distance or a squared mean-action distance depending on the actor parameterization.
The total SAW loss is the sum of value, low-level actor, flat actor, and waypoint losses.
In our experiments, recurrent refinement replaces only $V_\psi$ and its target backbone; the actors, goal representations, waypoint loss, and SAW weighting terms remain unchanged.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation-details}

\paragraph{Recurrent backbone.}
The reported recurrent models use a fixed-depth backbone with train-time depth $K$ and per-step inner capacity $m$.
For an input vector $x$, the module first projects $x$ to a hidden state $h^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h}$.
Each outer refinement step applies the same inner update stack, so the main channel-update weights are shared across outer steps.
The refinement index still enters through lightweight step-specific parameters: a learned step embedding $E_k\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ and a learned per-step LayerScale parameter $\alpha_k$.
Within one outer step, the $m$ inner SwiGLU blocks have separate parameters.
Concretely, for inner block $j$ we apply optional pre-LayerNorm, compute
\[
\Delta_{k,j}(h)=W_{o,j}\left(\mathrm{silu}(W_{a,j}(\mathrm{LN}_j(h)+E_k))\odot W_{b,j}(\mathrm{LN}_j(h)+E_k)\right),
\]
and update $h\leftarrow h+\Delta_{k,j}(h)$.
After the $m$ inner blocks, the proposed refined state is mixed with the previous outer state using the step-specific LayerScale parameter initialized to $10^{-2}$:
\[
h^{(k+1)} = h^{(k)}+\alpha_k(\tilde h^{(k)}-h^{(k)}).
\]
The final hidden state is mapped to the required output dimension by a linear projection.
The recurrent module has no auxiliary training loss, learned halting, or recurrent actor readout; hidden-drift quantities used in diagnostics are logged only for analysis.

\section{Computational Resources}\label{apd:compute-resources}
We tested the training jobs on NVIDIA 3090, 4090, H20, and H100 GPUs. Most reported experiments were run on 96GB H20 GPUs, with two independent training jobs typically sharing one GPU.

Training durations vary depending on the specific experimental settings. When running two training tasks concurrently on a single card, reproducing the baseline model typically takes 2--3 hours, while reproducing our proposed model with four refinement steps requires 3--4 hours. Runs with up to eight refinement steps take up to roughly 6 hours.
The main result table contains 21 algorithm-dataset rows over eight seeds; diagnostic, sweep, ablation, and compute tables use four seeds unless stated otherwise, so the aggregate reproduction cost can be estimated by multiplying these per-run durations by the corresponding number of rows and seeds.
Preliminary exploratory runs were used during development but are not required to reproduce the reported tables and figures.

Evaluation can be executed on either a GPU or a CPU. Completing one evaluation suite with five goals typically takes 2--3 minutes on a GPU and 20--25 minutes on a CPU.

\section{Broader Impacts}\label{broader impact}

This work contributes to improving offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, which is a foundational component of modern autonomous decision-making systems. By enhancing the ability of agents to learn effective value representations from static datasets through iterative refinement, our method improves the reliability and data efficiency of policy learning without requiring additional online interaction.

These improvements are particularly relevant to embodied intelligence and general agent systems, where real-world data collection is often expensive, slow, or unsafe. More effective offline learning algorithms can therefore facilitate the development of safer and more scalable embodied agents, including robotics systems, navigation agents, and other sequential decision-making applications that must operate under limited or pre-collected data regimes.

From a broader societal perspective, advances in data-efficient and reliable reinforcement learning can support the development of intelligent systems that assist humans in complex environments, potentially benefiting areas such as automation, logistics, and assistive robotics. By reducing reliance on unsafe exploration and improving generalization from existing datasets, such methods may contribute to safer deployment of learning-based systems in real-world scenarios.

We do not identify direct negative societal impacts specific to the proposed method.
